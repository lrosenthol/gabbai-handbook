# Torah Reading / קְרִיאַת הַתּוֹרָה

## Overview

The public reading of the Torah (*keriat ha-Torah*) is performed from a handwritten parchment scroll (*sefer Torah*).  The gabbai is responsible for coordinating every aspect of the reading, from removing the scroll from the ark to its return.

## The Number of Aliyot (עֲלִיּוֹת)

The number of Torah portions (*aliyot*) called varies by day. The following table documents the standard number of aliyot for the day/category.  It also notes (with +1) when the extra maftir/מַפְטִיר reading is added (for the person who will read the haftarah/הַפְטָרָה).  A gabbai may also call additional aliyot under certain circumstances ([TBD]).

| Day                                  | Number of *Aliyot* |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------ |
| Shabbat Morning                      | 7 + 1              |
| Yom Kippur Morning                   | 6 + 1              |
| Yom Tov (three Pilgrimage Festivals) | 5                  |
| Rosh Hashanah                        | 5                  |
| Rosh Chodesh / Chol Ha-Moed          | 4                  |
| Monday / Thursday / Shabbat Mincha   | 3                  |
| Yom Kippur Mincha                    | 2 + 1              |

## Calling an *Oleh* 

When calling a person to the Torah, the gabbai announces the *aliyah* in Hebrew.  The traditional formula includes the person's Hebrew name and father's name (*ben* for a man, *bat* for a woman):

יַעֲמֹד [שֵׁם] בֶּן [שֵׁם הָאָב] לַעֲלִיָּה [מִסְפַּר]

The gabbai should know in advance the Hebrew names of those receiving honours.  It is good practice to confirm names before the service.

## Calling the Maftir (מַפְטִיר)

The *maftir* is the person called to the final, additional reading, who then reads (or chants) the *haftarah*/הַפְטָרָה from the Prophets.  For the laws of who may receive maftir, see *Maftir* under Laws of Aliyot.

### When There Is a Maftir

A separate maftir is called on Shabbat morning, Yom Tov, Rosh Hashanah, and Yom Kippur morning, i.e., whenever a haftarah follows the reading.  At *Mincha* on a public fast day (Tzom Gedaliah, Asarah b'Tevet, Ta'anit Esther, Shiv'ah Asar b'Tammuz, and Tisha B'Av), at Yom Kippur *Mincha*, and on Tisha B'Av morning, there is no extra aliyah.  The third oleh is the maftir and reads the haftarah after his aliyah.[^tr1]

::: note-sephardic
**Sephardic custom:** The haftarah at fast-day *Mincha* comes from the *Rama*'s gloss.  The Shulchan Aruch itself mentions only the Torah reading.  Many Sephardic communities therefore read no haftarah at *Mincha* on an ordinary fast day.  At such a reading the third oleh is not a maftir.  Follow the established custom of the congregation.
:::

### Before Calling the Maftir

1. **Confirm the maftir before the service.**  Make sure he knows which haftarah is being read (watch for special haftarot on Rosh Chodesh, the Four Parshiyot, and other special Shabbatot) and that he is prepared to read it and say its blessings.
2. **Half-Kaddish (*chatzi kaddish*).**  After the last required aliyah ends, the reader says half-Kaddish, and only then is the maftir called.  Because the maftir is not one of the required aliyot, the Kaddish marks the end of the obligatory reading.[^tr2]
3. **When a second scroll is used.**  On Yom Tov, Shabbat Rosh Chodesh, Shabbat Chanukah, and the Four Parshiyot, the maftir is read from a second *sefer Torah*.[^tr3]  The common Ashkenazic order is:
    - Place the second scroll on the bimah next to the first, already rolled to the maftir reading.
    - The reader says half-Kaddish with both scrolls on the bimah.
    - Call *hagbah* and *gelilah* for the first scroll.
    - Call the maftir to the second scroll.

    When three scrolls are used, the maftir reads from the last one.[^tr4]  The common cases are:
    - Shabbat Rosh Chodesh Tevet during Chanukah (weekly portion, Rosh Chodesh, Chanukah).
    - Parashat Shekalim on Shabbat Rosh Chodesh Adar (weekly portion, Rosh Chodesh, Shekalim), and likewise Parashat HaChodesh on Shabbat Rosh Chodesh Nisan.
    - Simchat Torah (*Vezot Haberachah*, *Bereshit*, then maftir).

### The Announcement

The maftir is called with the standard formula, using the word *maftir* in place of the aliyah number:

יַעֲמֹד [שֵׁם] בֶּן [שֵׁם הָאָב] מַפְטִיר

If the maftir is a Kohen or Levi, add *ha-Kohen* or *ha-Levi* as for any other aliyah.  For a *bar mitzvah* boy many congregations announce:

יַעֲמֹד הַבָּחוּר הַבַּר מִצְוָה [שֵׁם] בֶּן [שֵׁם הָאָב] מַפְטִיר

### Who Typically Receives Maftir

Maftir is customarily given to a *bar mitzvah* boy on the Shabbat of his bar mitzvah (see *Precedence Among Yisraelim* under Laws of Aliyot).  A mourner for a parent customarily takes maftir during the twelve months of mourning.[^tr5]  Many communities also give maftir to someone observing a *yahrzeit*.

Because the maftir also reads the haftarah, the gabbai must not, *lechatchilah*, call someone who cannot say it.  If such a person was called, another may say the haftarah in his place.[^tr6]

A minor may be called for maftir, but Ashkenazic custom avoids calling a minor for maftir, at least *lechatchilah*, for Parashat Zachor and Parashat Parah, on the first day of Shavuot, on the seventh day of Pesach, and on Shabbat Shuvah.[^tr7]

After the maftir reading, the second (or only) scroll is lifted and wrapped (see below).  The maftir does not begin the haftarah until the wrapping is finished, so that the *golel* can also listen.[^tr8]

::: note-sephardic
**Sephardic custom:** Sephardic communities perform *hagbah* *before* the reading rather than after it.[^tr9]  So only the wrapping and the return of the scroll remain to be completed before the haftarah begins.
:::

## Correcting Errors

The gabbai must follow the reading carefully in a *tikkun* (a printed book with both the vowelised and unvowelised text side by side) and correct any significant error (*taut*) by the reader (*ba'al korei*).

An error is considered significant (*me'akev*) if it changes the meaning of the text.  Minor mispronunciations that do not affect meaning need not be corrected publicly, though the gabbai may quietly guide the reader.
